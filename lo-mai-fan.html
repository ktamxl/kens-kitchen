Stir-fried Glutinous Rice

生炒臘味糯米飯
Serves4
Prep20 min + 3 hr soak
Cook35 minutes
DifficultyHard

This is the dish Hong Kong cooks order in restaurants and almost never make at home: raw glutinous rice, stir-fried from scratch with the fat of preserved sausage and cured pork, liquid added a spoonful at a time, thirty minutes of stirring without stopping. There is no shortcut that tastes the same. The rice has to drink the fat slowly, grain by grain, until it turns translucent and chewy and still falls apart under the chopsticks. Do it once and you understand why it costs what it costs on a winter menu.

01

Ingredients

The Rice

Glutinous rice 糯米200 g
Water or light stock, for sprinkling100–150 ml
Glutinous rice, not sushi rice. It is opaque and chalky before soaking, and it is the only rice that goes properly sticky-chewy. Short-grain japonica will cook, but it will not give you this texture.

The Cured Meats & Dried Goods

Preserved sausage 臘腸 — about 40 g each1
Preserved pork belly 臘肉 — a 200 g piece¼ piece
Dried shrimp 蝦米38 g
Dried shiitake mushrooms 冬菇4
Fried peanuts 炸花生a small handful
Buy the sausage with visible fat. The white flecks are the point — they render into the oil that cooks the rice. A lean sausage gives you a dry wok and a dry dish.

The Seasoning

Light soy sauce 生抽1 tsp
Oyster sauce 蠔油1 tbsp
Dark soy sauce 老抽1 tsp
Sugar 糖½ tsp
Sesame oil 麻油1 tsp
Spring onion 蔥, sliced2
Mix the sauces in a bowl first. Once the rice is cooked you have seconds before it starts to catch — there is no time to open four bottles at the wok.
02

Method

  1. 1
    Soak the Rice — 3 Hours
    Cover the glutinous rice with cold water and leave it for 3 hours — longer is fine, overnight is easier. Drain it in a sieve for at least 15 minutes, then spread it on a tray to dry at the edges. The grains should look damp, not wet.
    ⚠️ Rice straight out of the water steams instead of frying. If it is still dripping, it will clump the moment it hits the wok.
  2. 2
    Soak the Dried Goods — and Keep the Water
    Soak the shiitake and the dried shrimp separately in cold water for 3 hours. Keep the shrimp water — that is the liquid you will add to the rice, and it is where most of the flavour comes from. Meanwhile, blanch the sausage and pork belly in boiling water for 2 minutes: it washes off the surface grit and makes them far easier to slice.
  3. 3
    Cut Everything Small and Even
    Slice the sausage into thin coins on the bias, dice the pork belly into 1 cm cubes, and cut the squeezed-dry mushrooms and shrimp into pieces about the size of a rice grain. Everything goes into the rice, so anything too big will sit in the bowl like a lump.
  4. 4
    Render the Fat First
    Start the pork belly in a cold wok over low heat and let the fat run — this takes 4–5 minutes and you should not rush it. Add the sausage, then the mushrooms and shrimp, and fry until fragrant. Scoop the solids out and leave every drop of oil behind. That oil is what keeps the rice from sticking.
  5. 5
    Coat Every Grain
    Turn the heat to low and tip in the drained rice. Stir constantly for 3–4 minutes until every grain is glossy and coated in the cured-meat fat. Nothing is cooking yet — this step is purely about building a barrier between rice and metal.
    ⚠️ A non-stick wok will not survive this. Use a seasoned iron or carbon-steel wok, and keep the heat low until the rice is coated.
  6. 6
    Add Liquid a Spoonful at a Time — 30 Minutes
    Raise the heat to medium. When the rice starts to feel sticky and heavy, sprinkle in 1–2 tbsp of the shrimp water and stir it through. Wait until it turns sticky again before adding more. Repeat for 25–30 minutes, always stirring and scraping the base, until the grains are translucent, chewy and separate. You will use 100–150 ml in total.
    ⚠️ Never pour the liquid in at once. Rice that is given too much water at the start turns to porridge and never recovers.
  7. 7
    Season, Rest, Serve
    Return the sausage, pork, mushrooms and shrimp to the wok. Add the light soy, oyster sauce, dark soy and sugar and toss until the colour is even. Fold through the spring onion, drizzle the sesame oil, scatter the peanuts, then cover the wok, take it off the heat and let it rest for 3–4 minutes. Serve in bowls, or packed into a bamboo steamer the way the restaurants do.
03

Chef's Notes

🍚

Why Fry It Raw

Steaming the rice first and frying it after is faster and gives you something closer to ordinary fried rice. Frying from raw keeps the grains separate and lets them drink the cured-meat fat slowly. That is the whole difference, and it is why the dish takes half an hour of stirring.

🔥

Iron, Not Non-Stick

Thirty minutes of stirring over medium heat is beyond what a non-stick coating will take, and the coating is exactly what you are trying to avoid needing. A well-seasoned carbon-steel or cast-iron wok, hot and oily, will not stick.

💧

The Shrimp Water Is the Stock

The water the dried shrimp soaked in is salty, sweet and deeply savoury. Use it instead of plain water and the rice tastes of something. If you skipped the shrimp, use a light chicken stock — never water with extra soy.

🥢

The Rest Finishes the Centre

Take the wok off the heat, cover it, and wait four minutes. The residual steam finishes the middle of each grain while the outside stays firm. Serve it straight from the wok and the rice is chewier than it should be.

❄️

Better the Next Day, Steamed

It reheats beautifully — a bowl covered with a plate over simmering water for 8 minutes brings it back. Day-old rice is firmer and separates more cleanly, which some people prefer.

Scaling This Recipe

Doubling works, but only with a bigger wok — and it will not be twice as fast. Give it ten more minutes and stir in the same rhythm.
