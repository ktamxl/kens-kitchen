Ratatouille (Confit Byaldi)

普羅旺斯雜菜煲
Serves4–6
Prep~45 minutes
Bake375°F · ~45 min
DifficultyIntermediate

The dish from the film — confit byaldi, the ratatouille Remy serves at the end of Ratatouille, created for the movie by Chef Thomas Keller. It isn't a stew. Paper-thin rounds of zucchini, squash, eggplant and tomato are shingled tightly in an oval pan over a slow-cooked piperade of charred red pepper and tomato, then baked under parchment until the vegetables give way. Fiddly, yes — and the payoff is a plate that looks engineered but tastes sweet, soft and deeply savoury. The secret is underneath: the sauce.

01

Ingredients

For the Piperade Sauce

Red bell peppers2
Tomatoes (Campari, Roma or vine)1 lb
Olive oil2 tbsp
Shallots, large, peeled and roughly cut2
Garlic cloves, roughly chopped4
Red wine (Cabernet Sauvignon preferred)½ cup
Thyme sprigs4
Basil1 sprig
Kosher salt1 tsp
Granulated sugar2 tsp
Black peppera few cracks
Char the peppers and peel the tomatoes if you can. Those two steps are what give the piperade its depth — skip them and the sauce is flatter. No wine? Unsalted stock works.

For the Vegetables

Roma tomatoes, sliced ⅛″ width-wise4–5
Squash, sliced ⅛″ width-wise1–2
Zucchini, sliced ⅛″ width-wise1–2
Chinese or Japanese eggplant, sliced ⅛″ width-wise1
Olive oil, plus more for drizzling2 tbsp
Kosher salt & cracked black pepperto taste
Choose vegetables of similar diameter. The closer they are in thickness, the neater the shingled spiral sits — and the prettier it looks coming out of the oven.
02

Instructions

  1. 1
    Char the Peppers
    Char them over an open flame, rotating with metal tongs, or wrap in foil and roast at 425°F for 15–20 minutes on a sheet pan. While still hot, drop them into a heatproof bowl and cover so they steam for 5–10 minutes; the skin slips off after that. Remove stem and seeds, then dice.
    ⚠️ Charred pepper skin can flake off and pop into the flame. Keep your hands back and use long tongs.
  2. 2
    Blanch and Peel the Tomatoes
    Bring a pot of water to the boil. Core the tomatoes and cut a shallow X in the bottom of each. Off the heat, drop them in for 10–15 seconds, lift out, and peel once cool enough to handle. Chop roughly.
  3. 3
    Build the Piperade
    Heat the olive oil in a medium saucepan over medium heat. Add the shallot and garlic and sauté until the shallot turns translucent, about 1 minute. Add the peppers, tomatoes, thyme and basil, sauté for another 30 seconds to a minute, then add the red wine. Bring to a simmer and let it reduce, stirring occasionally, for about 30 minutes — until at least three-quarters reduced. Discard the basil and thyme.
  4. 4
    Blend, Strain, Reduce
    Blend the mixture as smooth as you can with an immersion blender, then push it through a sieve. Return it to the pan, season with the sugar, salt and pepper, and reduce over medium-low until the sauce no longer looks watery — you should be able to draw a line through it and have it hold. Taste and adjust.
    ⚠️ Keep a lid on the pan, cracked open. This sauce bubbles and spits as it reduces.
  5. 5
    Slice the Vegetables
    Cut the tomatoes, squash, zucchini and eggplant into ⅛″ rounds, width-wise. A mandoline makes short work of the zucchini and squash — mind your fingers, the blade is vicious.
  6. 6
    Assemble the Shingle
    Put a sheet pan on the middle rack and preheat the oven to 375°F. Spread the piperade across a 12″ oval casserole pan and shake it level. Shingle the slices in alternating order from the edge to the centre, packed tightly, about ⅛″ to ⅙″ apart. If you want to plate it like the movie, hold back ¼ cup of sauce for the vinaigrette.
  7. 7
    Bake Under Parchment
    Drizzle the shingled vegetables with olive oil, then a little salt and pepper. Cover with a sheet of oval parchment and bake for about 45 minutes, until tender — a toothpick into the middle ring should meet just a little resistance, not raw crunch.
    ⚠️ Pan matters: a ceramic pan can need 5–10 minutes longer than copper. Judge by the vegetables, not the clock.
  8. 8
    Serve — Two Ways
    Hot, with a generous spoon of the piperade. Or plate it the way Remy did: whisk ¼ cup of the sauce with 2 tbsp olive oil, mixing only briefly so it keeps that separated, ribboned look, and spoon it around the stack.
03

Chef's Notes

🥒

Keep the Slices Even

⅛″ to ⅙″, all of them. Thicker slices take longer to cook, bend badly when you shingle them, and the finished dish looks lumpy instead of jewelled.

🍅

The Sauce Is the Dish

The vegetables are only sweet and light — the depth all comes from the piperade. Season it properly and reduce it further than feels necessary. A watery sauce makes a dull ratatouille.

🔪

Mandoline, Carefully

A mandoline turns twenty minutes of knife work into three. It also takes fingertips off without hesitation — use the guard, or a cut-resistant glove.

🫙

Storage

Keeps up to 5 days in the fridge once cooled, in an airtight container. Reheat in a pan or microwave. Note the vegetables release more moisture the longer they cook, so day-four leftovers are softer than day one.

Scaling This Recipe

Sauce scales straight up — the vegetable counts are approximate anyway, since it comes down to how much it takes to fill your pan.
